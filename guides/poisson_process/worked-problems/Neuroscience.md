# Neuroscience
A neuroscience lab records spikes generated by a neuron. Spikes follow a Poisson process at 20 spikes per
second. Each spike is independently classified as high-amplitude (probability 0.30) or low-amplitude (probability
0.70).  
(a) Determine the rates of the high- and low-amplitude spike processes.  
(b) Find P(exactly 4 high-amplitude spikes in 1 second).  
(c) Find P(no low-amplitude spikes in 0.5 seconds).  
(d) Find P(exactly 3 high-amplitude and exactly 5 low-amplitude spikes in the same 1 second).  


# Understanding Poisson processes and splitting

The key is to separate two kinds of randomness: **how many spikes occur** and **how each spike is classified**. A Poisson process models the first, while independent high/low labels let us split that process into two simpler Poisson processes.

## The fundamentals

### A rate is not a fixed count

“20 spikes per second” means an average rate of 20, not exactly 20 spikes every second. Let $N(t)$ be the total number of spikes in an interval of length $t$ seconds; then the Poisson-process model gives

$$
N(t)\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(20t).
$$

More generally, a process with rate $\lambda$ has a Poisson count with mean $\lambda t$ over an interval of length $t$. The probability of exactly $k$ events is ([Statistics LibreTexts](https://stats.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Probability_Theory/Probability_Mathematical_Statistics_and_Stochastic_Processes_(Siegrist)/14:_The_Poisson_Process/14.05:_Thinning_and_Superpositon)):

$$
\boxed{
P(N(t)=k)=\frac{e^{-\lambda t}(\lambda t)^k}{k!}
}
$$

The distinction to remember is **rate versus interval mean**. The rate $\lambda$ is measured in spikes per second, while $\lambda t$ is the expected number of spikes in the interval you are studying.

### Independent classification produces “thinning”

Suppose each event in a Poisson process of rate $\lambda$ is independently labeled high with probability $p$, and low with probability $1-p$. The Poisson thinning theorem says that the two resulting processes are themselves Poisson, with rates $\lambda p$ and $\lambda(1-p)$, and that they are independent ([Statistics LibreTexts](https://stats.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Probability_Theory/Probability_Mathematical_Statistics_and_Stochastic_Processes_(Siegrist)/14:_The_Poisson_Process/14.05:_Thinning_and_Superpositon)).

For this problem, define $H(t)$ and $L(t)$ as the high- and low-amplitude counts in the same $t$-second interval. We therefore have

$$
H(t)\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(20\cdot0.30\cdot t)
=\operatorname{Poisson}(6t),
$$

$$
L(t)\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(20\cdot0.70\cdot t)
=\operatorname{Poisson}(14t).
$$

## Applying the model

### (a) Rates of the two processes

Multiply the original rate by the probability of the corresponding label. As a check, the two rates should add back to the original rate:

$$
\boxed{\lambda_H=20(0.30)=6\text{ spikes/second}}
$$

$$
\boxed{\lambda_L=20(0.70)=14\text{ spikes/second}}
$$

Indeed, $6+14=20$. These are average rates, not guaranteed counts in each second.

### (b) Exactly 4 high-amplitude spikes in 1 second

We only care about the high-amplitude process, so use its rate of 6 rather than the total rate of 20. Over one second, its expected count is $6(1)=6$:

$$
H(1)\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(6).
$$

Substitute $k=4$ into the Poisson formula:

$$
P(H(1)=4)
=\frac{e^{-6}6^4}{4!}
=\boxed{0.133853\text{ approximately}}.
$$

That is about **13.39%**. There is no restriction on how many low-amplitude spikes occur during that second.

### (c) No low-amplitude spikes in 0.5 seconds

The low-amplitude rate is 14 per second, but the interval is only half a second. Thus the expected count for this interval is $14(0.5)=7$:

$$
L(0.5)\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(7).
$$

“No spikes” means a count of zero, so

$$
P(L(0.5)=0)
=\frac{e^{-7}7^0}{0!}
=e^{-7}
=\boxed{0.000911882\text{ approximately}}.
$$

That is about **0.0912%**, which is small because the expected low-amplitude count is 7. This event still allows high-amplitude spikes to occur.

### (d) Exactly 3 high-amplitude and 5 low-amplitude spikes in the same second

Here we need both count conditions to hold. The independence supplied by Poisson thinning lets us multiply their probabilities, even though the observation intervals are the same:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(H(1)=3,\ L(1)=5)
&=P(H(1)=3)\,P(L(1)=5)\\[2mm]
&=\left(\frac{e^{-6}6^3}{3!}\right)
  \left(\frac{e^{-14}14^5}{5!}\right)\\[2mm]
&=\frac{e^{-20}6^3\,14^5}{3!\,5!}\\[2mm]
&=\boxed{0.000332561\text{ approximately}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

That is about **0.0333%**. Notice that we used the full one-second interval for each process; we do not divide the second between the two types.

## Why Poisson and binomial both appear

There is another way to understand part (d): exactly 3 high and 5 low means exactly 8 total spikes, with exactly 3 of those labeled high. Given that 8 spikes occurred, the high-amplitude count is binomial because there are now 8 independent classifications, each with high probability $0.30$ ([Statistics LibreTexts](https://stats.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Probability_Theory/Probability_Mathematical_Statistics_and_Stochastic_Processes_(Siegrist)/14:_The_Poisson_Process/14.05:_Thinning_and_Superpositon)).

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(H(1)=3,L(1)=5)
&=P(N(1)=8)\,P(H(1)=3\mid N(1)=8)\\[2mm]
&=\underbrace{\frac{e^{-20}20^8}{8!}}_{\text{exactly 8 total spikes}}
\underbrace{\binom{8}{3}(0.30)^3(0.70)^5}_{\text{exactly 3 of those 8 are high}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

This simplifies to the same answer:

$$
\frac{e^{-20}20^8}{8!}
\frac{8!}{3!\,5!}(0.30)^3(0.70)^5
=
\frac{e^{-20}6^3\,14^5}{3!\,5!}.
$$

This also resolves an apparent puzzle about independence. With the total fixed at 8, knowing there are 3 high spikes forces there to be 5 low spikes, so those conditional counts are dependent; without fixing the total, the joint probability factors into two Poisson probabilities, as the algebra above shows.

The reusable method is: **split the rate, multiply by the interval length, then apply the Poisson formula**. Use a binomial model when you condition on a known total number of spikes and ask how those spikes are classified.




# Why Poisson thinning works

The key idea is that the number of spikes is random, but **once you know how many occurred, their classifications follow a binomial distribution**. We will combine those two distributions and show that the result is another Poisson distribution.

## Start with the two layers of randomness

Consider an interval of length $t$ seconds, and write its expected total count as $\mu=\lambda t$. Let $N$ be the total number of spikes and $H$ the number classified as high-amplitude.

The total count is Poisson:

$$
N\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\mu),
\qquad
P(N=n)=\frac{e^{-\mu}\mu^n}{n!}.
$$

Now suppose we are told that exactly $n$ spikes occurred. Each of those $n$ spikes independently has probability $p$ of being high, so

$$
H\mid N=n\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(n,p).
$$

Consequently,

$$
P(H=k\mid N=n)
=\binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}.
$$

This is the distinction: **the total is not fixed in advance**, but conditioning on it temporarily gives us a fixed number of classification trials. We then average over all possible totals.

## Derive the high-amplitude count distribution

### Account for every possible total

To have exactly $k$ high-amplitude spikes, we could have $k$ total spikes, or $k+1$, or $k+2$, and so on. These possibilities are mutually exclusive, so the law of total probability gives

$$
P(H=k)
=
\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}
P(H=k\mid N=n)\,P(N=n).
$$

Substitute the binomial and Poisson formulas:

$$
P(H=k)
=
\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}
\binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}
\frac{e^{-\mu}\mu^n}{n!}.
$$

### Simplify the expression

Expand the binomial coefficient using

$$
\binom{n}{k}=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}.
$$

The $n!$ terms cancel, leaving

$$
P(H=k)
=
e^{-\mu}
\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}
\frac{\mu^n p^k(1-p)^{n-k}}{k!(n-k)!}.
$$

Next, split $\mu^n=\mu^k\mu^{n-k}$ and move everything that does not depend on $n$ outside the sum:

$$
P(H=k)
=
e^{-\mu}\frac{(\mu p)^k}{k!}
\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}
\frac{[\mu(1-p)]^{n-k}}{(n-k)!}.
$$

### Recognize the exponential series

Set $j=n-k$, so the sum now starts at $j=0$. We can then use the exponential-series identity

$$
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^j}{j!}=e^x.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(H=k)
&=
e^{-\mu}\frac{(\mu p)^k}{k!}
e^{\mu(1-p)}\\[2mm]
&=
\boxed{\frac{e^{-\mu p}(\mu p)^k}{k!}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

That is exactly the Poisson probability formula with mean $\mu p$. Since $\mu=\lambda t$,

$$
\boxed{H(t)\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda pt)}.
$$

For your neuron, this gives

$$
H(t)\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(20\cdot0.30\cdot t)
=\operatorname{Poisson}(6t).
$$

Replacing $p$ with $1-p=0.70$ gives the low-amplitude result. Thus,

$$
L(t)\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(14t).
$$

## Why the two counts are independent

Showing that both counts are Poisson does not, by itself, prove independence. To establish that, we calculate their joint probability and see whether it factors.

Let $L=N-H$. Having $H=h$ and $L=\ell$ requires $h+\ell$ total spikes, exactly $h$ of which are high:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(H=h,L=\ell)
&=
P(N=h+\ell)\,
P(H=h\mid N=h+\ell)\\[2mm]
&=
\frac{e^{-\mu}\mu^{h+\ell}}{(h+\ell)!}
\frac{(h+\ell)!}{h!\ell!}
p^h(1-p)^\ell\\[2mm]
&=
\underbrace{\frac{e^{-\mu p}(\mu p)^h}{h!}}_{P(H=h)}
\underbrace{\frac{e^{-\mu(1-p)}[\mu(1-p)]^\ell}{\ell!}}_{P(L=\ell)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

We obtained

$$
\boxed{P(H=h,L=\ell)=P(H=h)P(L=\ell)},
$$

which is precisely the definition of independence for these discrete counts. This is why multiplying the probabilities in part (d) was valid.

There is one final distinction between a Poisson count and a Poisson process. Counts in disjoint intervals remain independent because they come from independent original Poisson increments and independent classifications; together with the interval distributions derived above, this establishes the two thinned Poisson processes.

The main takeaway is that a **Poisson-distributed number of independent Bernoulli classifications produces a Poisson-distributed number of successes**. If the total number of trials were fixed instead, the success count would remain binomial.
